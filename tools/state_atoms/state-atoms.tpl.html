State Atoms at k = 18

Can whole states, with the oversized ones cut into pieces, replace the zip as the atomic unit for carving districts of roughly equal opportunity? Yes, but the cost is set almost entirely by how finely the four big groups are cut. At three pieces for California it exceeds the incumbency premium; at five it is a tenth of it.

Where the model stands today

Stage 1 draws k balanced districts over 3,748 zips. It is a transportation LP at fixed centers, solved for its duals, which makes each district a convex power cell. centers.py states its own contract plainly: "No contiguity, no adjacency: this module is pure functions on arrays."

That omission is not an oversight. It is forced. The instance carries a real rook-adjacency graph over sold zips, and on v2 that graph has 862 connected components across 3,748 nodes, 516 of them single zips. The largest component holds 13.5 % of opportunity, and 47.6 % of opportunity sits in components worth under 1 % each. A contiguity constraint on that graph is either vacuous or infeasible, so stage 1 ignores it and buys coherence indirectly, from the geometry of the power diagram.

Geometry has its own cost. Stage 1 needs a coordinate per zip, and 41 zips in the delivered k = 18 draw have none; they are placed afterwards by a state-plurality fallback in channel.place_by_state.

Finding

Contracting the instance's zip graph to states does not produce the US state map. It produces 10 edges and 42 components, with 36 states isolated, because the underlying zip graph is shattered and most states touch their neighbours only in unsold territory.

So a state-atom model cannot derive its adjacency from the instance. It has to use the true state rook graph, roughly 109 edges over the lower 48. That graph is public, fixed, and one connected piece. tools/instance_export/build_adjacency.py already downloads the TIGER state shapefile it would come from.

It does say something about how stage 2 will have to work, since no districting of any grain can hand a firm a clean region that does not exist in the data.

The atom inventory

Every atom's opportunity, as a multiple of the 473.5 target. The question of whether states can serve as atoms is settled almost entirely by this one distribution: a handful of atoms carry more than a district's worth of opportunity and must be cut, and every other one fits inside a single district with room to spare.

An atom is a state or a group of states, not a state alone. Where a metro straddles a state line the two states have to be cut together, or the cut is drawn in the wrong place. New York and New Jersey are the first such group.

Cutting each oversized group into pieces no larger than one target gives 13 pieces from the four, so the model runs on 60 atoms for 18 districts. Grouping New York with New Jersey is what removes a piece: taken separately they are 1.794× and 1.037×, needing 2 and 2, and the second New Jersey piece would be a 3.7 % sliver with nowhere sensible to go.

Evidence

The current draw already fuses the two, without being told to. D01 is 373.6 New York plus 96.7 New Jersey out of 471.8, and D12 is 340.9 New Jersey plus 128.6 New York out of 471.8. Each is over 99 % metro mass from the two states combined. The power diagram found the tri-state metro on geometry alone; grouping the atoms just states it up front.

The remaining New York and New Jersey mass leaks into two neighbours: a New England district (New York 291.2, Connecticut 86.8, Massachusetts 47) and a mid-Atlantic one (Pennsylvania 251, Maryland 107, New York 54.3, New Jersey 43.6).

Worth deciding

Adding Connecticut makes the group 1,427.2, or 3.014× target. Three pieces of a tri-state block, each 0.5 % over a full district, with no spillover to negotiate with New England at all. New York plus New Jersey alone is 2.831×, so its three pieces each run 5.6 % light and have to draw the balance from a neighbour.

That is a cleaner block, and it is the sponsor's call rather than the model's, so it is left open here.

What the coarsening buys

Contiguity becomes a constraint the model can actually carry

On 61 atoms over one connected graph, contiguity is enforceable rather than decorative. The separator-cut machinery already written for the two-player engine in scip_tree.py applies directly, and at this size it is small work rather than the reason the approach was abandoned.

The Nash objective goes linear

Enumerate connected atom subsets whose mass falls in a band around the target, precompute log MS for each, and the districting becomes a set-partition program with a linear objective. That is an exact optimum with a certificate, not a heuristic draw with a bound attached afterwards. It is also the idiom the project already uses elsewhere: A1's charter is roster enumeration over band-constrained programs.

Geometry drops out entirely

No coordinates, no projection, no power cells, and no plurality fallback for the 41 zips the gazetteer has no point for. The model would read only M per atom and the state adjacency graph.

State fragmentation ends by construction

The delivered k = 18 draw splits 38 of 52 state codes across two or more districts. California and New York land in five districts each; Florida, Georgia, Nebraska, New Jersey, Ohio, Tennessee and Texas in four. Georgia is only 0.39× target and is still cut four ways. Under state atoms only the five oversized states are ever divided.

What the coarsening costs on balance

This is now measured rather than argued. Stage 1 maximises Σ log Mj, and at a fixed total that objective is exactly the balance objective, so the whole question reduces to one number: how close can 18 groups of atoms get to the perfectly balanced ceiling of 110.883247?

The partition below drops contiguity on purpose. Removing a constraint can only help, so each value is an upper bound on what any contiguous draw of that kind could reach. The point is to find out whether atom granularity is the obstacle before spending effort on the contiguity machinery.

Result

Balance is free at this granularity. Both split rules land within 0.00012 nats of the perfectly balanced ceiling, and both sit above the zip-level draw's own value, because that draw is not perfectly balanced either. For scale, the incumbency premium the programme exists to measure is 0.72 to 0.78 nats, roughly six thousand times larger than the worst gap here.

The reason is that log is flat near its argument and the atom set has a long tail of small states, from Illinois at 0.677× down to Wyoming at 0.002×, which is more than enough change to settle 18 districts onto the target.

Read it carefully

That table drops contiguity, so it isolates one thing only: whether atom size is the obstacle. It is not. The last row is the design point: refusing to split the oversized groups costs 3.87 nats, about five times the incumbency premium. Splitting them is not a convenience, it is the whole mechanism.

What that table does not answer is what a real, contiguous draw achieves at a given set of cuts. That is the next section, and it is where the cost actually appears.

The answer

Cutting California into 3 costs 0.864 nats. That is more than the entire incumbency premium of 0.72 to 0.78 nats, so at those cuts the coarsening gives up more value than the effect the programme exists to measure. Three pieces of California are 1.375× target each, and a piece above target can never be combined with anything, so it is stranded as an oversized district.

Cutting it into 5, with New York and New Jersey into 3 and Florida into 2, brings the cost to 0.094 nats, about an eighth of the premium. Texas at 2 needs no revision at any setting: its two pieces come out at 1.013× and 1.007× target.

Structural

Cutting New York and New Jersey into 2 severs New England from the country. Those six states reach the rest of the network only through New York, so once both New York pieces are too big to absorb anything, CT MA ME NH RI VT becomes its own graph component and must form a district of 0.434× target, the worst in any draw here. A third New York piece at 0.944× repairs it, and the atom graph returns to a single component.

Method, and its limit

These draws come from seeded growth plus local search, where every move and swap is checked to keep both districts connected. They are not certified optimal. The exact set-partition route was tried first and abandoned on measurement: enumerating the connected subsets of the 47- and 54-atom components passes a quarter of a million columns, and at 177,000 columns HiGHS could not find even a feasible cover in 180 seconds.

The bound margin column is what stands in for a certificate. It is the same atom set partitioned with contiguity dropped, which can only do better, so the true contiguous optimum lies between the draw and the draw plus that margin. The ranking of the four cut settings is wider than every margin, so the comparison holds even though each individual value could improve.

Open decisions

Formulation

Hard atoms or soft?

A hard model makes every district an exact union of atoms, which is what delivers the certificate and the clean state story. A soft model treats state boundaries as a default the solver may cut where the nats justify it, which recovers value but gives up the small, exactly-solvable program.

Grouping

Which other states are cut together?

New York with New Jersey is settled. The same argument reaches other metros that straddle a line: Philadelphia over PA, NJ and DE; Chicago over IL, IN and WI; Kansas City over MO and KS; Washington over DC, MD and VA. None of those groups exceeds one district of opportunity, so grouping them changes nothing about splitting, only about whether the model is allowed to draw a boundary through a single labour market.

Geometry

How are the four oversized groups cut?

Three routes. Inherit the pieces the current v2 draw already carves inside each group, which costs nothing and guarantees the pieces are coherent, and which for New York and New Jersey hands back two near-complete districts already. Run k-means inside each group at the piece count above. Or take named sponsor regions through the existing --scenario path, which is defensible but waits on the A12 call.

Data

What happens to the 32 stateless zips?

They carry 33.4 M, about 0.4 % of the total, and the current draw scatters them across all 18 districts. A state-atom model has nowhere to put them without a rule: a 53rd floating atom, an attachment to a neighbour by their zip-graph edges, or a correction to the export.

Settle the cuts, or build the engine?

The cost is now known as a function of the cuts, so the remaining choice is how much of the 0.094-nat residual is worth chasing. Certifying optimality would need a working exact solver, which the set-partition attempt showed is not free at this size. The alternative is to accept the draw and ship it as a stage-1 engine under the base.py harness contract.
